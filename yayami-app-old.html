Ferments

Four live cultures to maintain: Kefir (ongoing), Skyr, Greek Yoghurt, and Sourdough Starter. Each has its own rhythm. Track batches, log when you started, and never run out again.

🥛 Kefir — Continuous Culture
Ongoing · Never starts from scratch

Kefir is the easiest ferment once you understand one rule: control the grain quantity, not the process. Keep 2–3 tablespoons of grains fermenting 1 litre of milk. Everything else follows. Best dairy option for Maxi — 70–80% of lactose is broken down during fermentation.

The grain management rule: Your active colony = 2–3 tbsp. No more. When grains exceed this, freeze or dry the excess immediately — do not let them accumulate. This is the only thing that went wrong last time.
Active grains needed
2–3 tbsp
Milk per batch
1 litre
Ferment time
24 hrs room temp
Yield
~950ml kefir
For Maxi
Low lactose ✓
1
The active cycle (every 2 days)
Pour 1 litre whole milk into a clean 1.5L glass jar. Add 2–3 tbsp kefir grains. Cover with a cloth or paper towel secured with a rubber band — not a sealed lid (CO2 needs to escape). Leave at room temperature (18–24°C) for 24 hours. The milk will thicken, smell pleasantly sour, and may separate slightly. Strain through a plastic sieve (never metal — it damages grains) into a drinking jar. The grains stay in the sieve — put them straight back into the jar with fresh milk.
⏱ 24 hrs fermentation
💡 Warmer kitchen (24°C+) = faster, thinner, more sour kefir in 18–20 hrs. Cooler (18°C) = 26–28 hrs, thicker, milder. Adjust to taste.
2
Fridge rest cycle (when you need a break)
Place grains in a jar, cover with fresh milk, seal with a lid, refrigerate. Feed once a week — pour off the milk (use it in bread dough or pancakes, it's mildly fermented), add fresh milk, back in fridge. Grains go dormant and barely grow. When you want active kefir again, take out of fridge, put in fresh milk at room temp — back to full activity within one cycle (24 hrs).
💡 This is your solution to the "too much kefir" problem. Two weeks in Corvallis, busy with school runs — grains go in the fridge. Zero stress.
3
Excess grain management — do this monthly
Once a month, measure your grains. If they exceed 4 tbsp, remove the excess immediately. Three options: (1) Blend into a smoothie — nutritious, undetectable. (2) Freeze: rinse grains in non-chlorinated water, pat dry, coat lightly in milk powder, freeze in a zip bag — viable for 3–6 months. (3) Dry: spread on baking paper, leave 2–3 days at room temp until hard and yellowish, store in a sealed jar — your permanent backup colony.
💡 Keep one frozen backup batch always. If your active grains ever die (rare but possible), you revive from frozen within 3–4 days.
4
Second fermentation — thicker, less sour, more fizzy
After straining, seal the finished kefir in a bottle and leave at room temperature another 4–8 hours before refrigerating. CO2 builds up, the kefir gets slightly fizzy, tangier, and thicker — more like drinking yoghurt. This is optional but makes it significantly more palatable for people (like Caleb) who find plain kefir too sour. Then refrigerate — keeps 1 week.

🫙 Skyr — Strained Icelandic Yoghurt
Very high protein · Thick · Mild

Skyr is essentially yoghurt that has been strained until almost all the whey is removed — leaving a thick, creamy, very high-protein product (15–17g per 150g serving vs ~8g for regular yoghurt). Store-bought skyr costs $6–8 per 500g. Your homemade version costs about $1.20 for the same amount. Save the whey — it goes into bread dough.

Milk needed
2 litres
Yield after straining
~700–800g skyr
Protein per 150g
~15–17g
Cost
~$1.20 total
Keeps
7–10 days
1
Heat milk (Friday evening)
Pour 2 litres whole milk into a heavy pot. Heat slowly to 85°C (185°F), stirring constantly to prevent scorching on the bottom. Hold at 85°C for 5 minutes — this denatures the whey proteins and gives skyr its characteristically thick, smooth texture rather than the looser texture of regular yoghurt. Remove from heat.
💡 Use a thermometer. 85°C is significantly hotter than yoghurt's 43°C — it's a genuine heat treatment, not just warming.
2
Cool to 43°C and inoculate
Let the milk cool to exactly 43°C — this takes 30–45 minutes. Whisk in 4 tbsp of your previous skyr batch (or plain full-fat yoghurt as starter for the first batch). The cultures are killed above 46°C, so do not rush this step. Pour into clean glass jars.
⏱ 30–45 min cooling
3
Incubate 8–10 hours
Wrap jars in a thick towel and place in your oven with only the light on (typically 28–32°C). Or use the Instant Pot yoghurt setting if yours has one. Leave completely undisturbed for 8–10 hours. The result will be a loose, wobbly yoghurt-like set. This is correct — straining will transform it.
⏱ 8–10 hrs overnight
💡 Do not open the oven or disturb the jars during incubation. Temperature fluctuation prevents proper setting.
4
Strain (Saturday morning) — this is what makes it skyr
Line a colander with cheesecloth or a clean cotton cloth. Pour in the set yoghurt. Place over a bowl and refrigerate. Strain 4–6 hours for thick Greek-style. Strain 8–12 hours for true skyr texture — very thick, almost spreadable, very high protein. The liquid that drains off is whey: save every drop in a jar. Use it in bread dough instead of water, in pancake batter, in oatmeal, or drink it straight (high in protein and B vitamins).
⏱ 4–12 hrs straining
💡 The longer you strain, the thicker and more protein-dense the result. For Maxi's protein goals, strain the full 12 hours.

🥄 Greek Yoghurt — Strained but Lighter than Skyr
Creamier · Less sour · Versatile

Greek yoghurt is the same process as skyr but strained for less time — 2–4 hours instead of 8–12. The result is creamier, less dense, slightly less sour. Better for raita, tzatziki, as a sour cream substitute, or for anyone who finds skyr too thick. Keep both: use skyr for breakfast and protein, Greek yoghurt for cooking and sauces.

Milk needed
1.5 litres
Yield
~600–700g
Protein per 150g
~12–14g
Cost
~$0.90 total
Keeps
10–14 days
1
Same process as Skyr — steps 1 through 3 are identical
Heat 1.5L milk to 85°C, hold 5 min. Cool to 43°C. Whisk in 3 tbsp starter (previous Greek yoghurt batch, or skyr, or plain yoghurt). Pour into jars. Incubate 8–10 hours wrapped in a towel in the oven with the light on.
⏱ 8–10 hrs overnight
2
Strain 2–4 hours only — stop here for Greek style
Line a colander with cheesecloth, pour in the set yoghurt, refrigerate. Check after 2 hours — it should be noticeably thicker but still creamy and spoonable, not firm. Stop straining when it reaches your preferred consistency. 2 hours = light Greek style. 4 hours = thick, almost like sour cream. Beyond 4 hours you're heading into skyr territory.
⏱ 2–4 hrs straining
💡 Greek yoghurt is your cooking yoghurt. It won't split in hot sauces the way thin yoghurt does. Use it in the biryani raita, in marinades, as a sour cream substitute on the stew, stirred into dal for richness.
3
Differentiate your two strains
Label your jars clearly — skyr vs Greek yoghurt. They look identical but behave differently in recipes. Keep the skyr for eating straight with honey/granola/fruit and for Maxi's protein. Keep the Greek yoghurt for cooking, raita, tzatziki, and spreading on bread. You can make both from the same Friday-night incubation batch — just split the yoghurt into two portions and strain each for different durations.
💡 One Friday evening batch of 3.5L milk = both your skyr and Greek yoghurt for the week. Strain half for 12 hrs (skyr), half for 3 hrs (Greek). Efficient.

🌾 Sourdough Starter — Your Perpetual Culture
Keep 20–30g always · Never use it all

You never start from scratch again. You always keep 20–30g in a jar — that's your permanent culture. Before every bread bake, you feed it to build it up to the quantity the recipe needs, use most of it, and leave 20–30g behind. That remainder is your next starter. Simple, permanent, costs almost nothing to maintain.

Keep always
20–30g
Jar size
500ml glass
Weekly feed
50g flour + 50g water
Fridge rest
Feed once/week
1
Baking week rhythm
Friday evening: take the jar out of the fridge. Discard or use everything except 20–30g. Add 100g bread flour + 100g water at 25°C. Stir vigorously, cover loosely, leave at room temperature overnight. By Saturday morning it should have doubled or tripled, smell pleasantly sour-yeasty, and be domed on top. Use 200g for the bread recipe. The remaining 20–30g goes back in the fridge immediately after you've mixed the dough.
⏱ 8–12 hrs feed cycle
2
Non-baking weeks — fridge maintenance
Leave it in the fridge. Feed once a week: discard all but 20–30g, add 50g flour + 50g water, stir, back in fridge. That's it. The cold slows the culture to near-dormancy. A well-maintained starter kept this way will last indefinitely — some bakeries have starters decades old.
💡 If you forget for 2 weeks and it develops a grey liquid layer on top — that's "hooch" (alcohol from over-fermentation). Pour it off, discard all but 20g, feed twice 12 hours apart at room temperature, and it's back to full strength.
3
The discard — don't waste it
The portion you remove before feeding (the "discard") is not garbage. It's mildly active starter with a pleasant sour flavour. Use it in: pancake batter (replace half the flour), roti dough (adds flavour and slight chew), flatbread, crackers, or oatmeal. Keep a second "discard jar" in the fridge and use it during the week for weekday pancakes or quick flatbreads. Zero waste.

German Bread Guide

Two freestanding sourdough loaves — no tin, ever. Baked directly on a preheated stone or heavy steel sheet. Target: elongated Batard shape, width ~60% of length, height ~10cm, shattering crust. German-bakery quality or don't bother.

What makes these loaves stand up — the four non-negotiables:
① Surface tension: The shaped loaf must feel like a drum skin. Slack surface = spreads sideways. No exceptions.
② Baking stone or heavy steel preheated 45+ min at 250°C. Instant bottom heat kicks off oven spring before the crust sets.
③ Steam — first 15 min: Pour 200ml boiling water into a hot metal tray on the bottom rack. Steam keeps the crust elastic so the loaf keeps rising. Without it the crust locks too early and you get a flat brick.
④ Score at 30–45° angle: One deep slash along the length, blade angled not vertical. This directs the oven spring upward along the cut, not randomly sideways.
🌾 Weizensauerteigbrot — Freestanding Pure Wheat Sourdough
Freestanding Batard · ~900g · 10cm height

Pure wheat sourdough with a wide-open, chewy crumb and a crust so thick it shatters when you cut it. The high-protein bread flour gives you the gluten strength to hold a freestanding 10cm loaf without any support. This is your lightest, most versatile bread — great with cheese, jam, eggs, or dal.

Active time
30 min
Total time
18–22 hrs
Cost
~$0.65/loaf
Hydration
72%
Keeps
3–4 days
1
Feed the starter (Friday evening)
Mix 50g existing starter + 100g strong bread flour + 100g water at 25°C. Stir well, cover loosely, leave at room temperature 8–12 hours. It must be at peak activity — domed on top, full of bubbles, and passes the float test (a spoonful dropped in water floats). A weak starter is the number one cause of flat bread.
⏱ 8–12 hrs overnight
💡 Float test: drop a teaspoon of starter in a glass of water. If it floats, it's ready. If it sinks, wait another hour.
2
Autolyse — flour and water only (Saturday morning)
Combine 450g strong bread flour + 50g whole wheat flour + 360g water at 28°C. Mix with your hand until no dry flour remains — about 2 minutes. Cover and rest 45 minutes untouched. This step alone develops 60–70% of your gluten with zero effort and makes the dough far more extensible for shaping.
⏱ 45 min rest
💡 Use a thermometer for the water. At 28°C the whole dough will be roughly 24–25°C — the ideal bulk fermentation temperature. Cold water stalls everything.
3
Incorporate starter and salt
Add 200g active starter and 10g fine salt to the autolysed dough. Use the "pinch and fold" method: squeeze the starter through the dough with your fingers, fold, rotate, repeat for 4–5 minutes until fully incorporated and the dough feels cohesive again. It will look shaggy at first — keep going until smooth.
4
Bulk fermentation with stretch & fold (4–5 hrs)
Leave covered at 24–26°C. Perform 4 sets of stretch & folds spaced 30 minutes apart during the first 2 hours: wet your hand, grab one side of the dough, stretch it as high as it will go without tearing, fold it over to the opposite side. Rotate the bowl 90° and repeat — 4 folds = 1 set. After the 4th set, leave undisturbed until the dough has grown 60–75% and the surface shows domed bubbles.
⏱ 4–5 hrs total
💡 After the 4th fold set the dough should feel noticeably stronger — it will hold its shape briefly when you pull it, not sag immediately. That strength is what will hold up a 10cm freestanding loaf.
5
Pre-shape — the foundation of height
Lightly flour your work surface. Gently scrape the dough out — do not punch down or degas. Using a bench scraper, fold the edges underneath to form a rough oval. Drag it towards you across the surface — the friction tightens the top skin. Cover with a bowl or cloth and rest 25–30 minutes. This rest relaxes the gluten just enough to shape without tearing.
⏱ 25–30 min bench rest
6
Final shape — Batard (elongated oval)
Flip the dough so the smooth side is down. Fold the top third down to the centre, then the bottom third up over it (like a letter). Now fold the left side to the centre, then the right side over. Roll the whole package away from you into a tight oval log, sealing the seam with your palm. Drag it back towards you using the bench scraper — again the friction builds surface tension. The surface must feel tight like a drum. Place seam-side UP in a well-floured oval proofing basket (banneton) or a flour-lined bowl.
💡 Width target: roughly 60% of the length. A 28cm long batard should be about 17cm wide. If it spreads wider during shaping your dough is too slack — tighten more aggressively.
7
Cold proof overnight in the fridge
Cover the banneton with a shower cap or cling film and refrigerate 10–14 hours. Cold proofing slows fermentation, builds more complex flavour, and — critically — firms up the dough so it holds its shape when you flip it out onto the hot stone. A cold dough scores more cleanly and springs more dramatically than a room-temperature one.
⏱ 10–14 hrs fridge
8
Bake — preheat hard, steam aggressively
Place your baking stone or a heavy inverted steel sheet pan on the middle rack. Place an empty metal roasting tray on the bottom rack. Preheat at 250°C (480°F) for a MINIMUM of 45 minutes — the stone must be saturated with heat. Take the dough straight from the fridge. Flip it out onto a sheet of baking parchment. Score immediately with a razor blade or very sharp knife: one slash from end to end, blade angled at 30–45° (not vertical), 1.5–2cm deep. Slide onto the stone. Immediately pour 250ml of boiling water into the bottom tray and CLOSE THE OVEN FAST. Do not open for 15 minutes.
💡 The angle of the score is everything. A vertical cut gives you a round lip. A 45° angled cut gives you the classic "ear" — the flap that peels back and rises upward, adding another 1–2cm of height visually.
9
Finish without steam — build the crust
After 15 minutes, open the oven, remove the steam tray, and reduce temperature to 220°C. Bake a further 22–28 minutes until the crust is deep mahogany brown — not golden, not pale, genuinely dark. It should sound hollow when you knock the bottom. Internal temperature: 96–97°C. Transfer to a wire rack immediately and leave untouched for at least 1 hour — the crumb is still setting.
⏱ 37–43 min total bake
💡 For a thicker, harder crust: after baking, turn the oven off, crack the door open 5cm, and leave the loaf inside for 20 more minutes. The residual heat and airflow dries the crust further. This is how German bakeries get that almost-shattering crust.
⚖️ Mischbrot — Freestanding 50/50 Wheat-Rye Sourdough
Freestanding Batard · Denser crumb · Complex flavour

The most eaten bread in Germany. Darker, denser, more complex than pure wheat — a slightly sour, earthy flavour that pairs with everything from cheese to cold cuts to soup. The rye component weakens gluten, so the technique is more demanding: lower hydration, firmer pre-shape, and a slightly cooler final bake. A freestanding 10cm Mischbrot is achievable but requires precise shaping. Do not skip the pre-shape rest.

Active time
35 min
Total time
20–24 hrs
Cost
~$0.70/loaf
Hydration
74%
Keeps
4–5 days
1
Build the Roggensauer — rye pre-ferment (Thursday evening)
Mix 200g dark rye flour + 200g warm water (28°C) + 2 tbsp active wheat starter. Stir well, cover, and leave 12–14 hours at room temperature. By Friday morning it will be very bubbly, smell sharply sour, and have risen and begun to fall. This is your Roggensauer — the engine of the bread's flavour. Building the rye component separately and overnight gives far more complexity than mixing it all at once on baking day.
⏱ 12–14 hrs overnight
💡 The Roggensauer is ready when it smells sour-fruity and has visible bubbles throughout. If it smells alcoholic or like acetone, it over-fermented — start again. Use less starter or a cooler room next time.
2
Mix the final dough (Friday morning or Saturday morning)
Combine the full Roggensauer + 280g strong bread flour + 20g whole wheat flour + 150g water at 28°C + 80g active wheat starter + 11g fine salt. Mix thoroughly by hand for 4–5 minutes — the dough will be noticeably stickier and denser than pure wheat. This is correct. Hydration is kept lower (74%) precisely to compensate for the rye's weaker gluten, giving you a shapeable dough rather than a slack one.
💡 Do not add more water even if tempted. The rye absorbs water slowly — after 30 minutes of resting the dough will feel more manageable.
3
Bulk fermentation — gentler folds than wheat
Cover and leave at 24–26°C for 3.5–4.5 hours. Perform only 3 sets of stretch & folds in the first 90 minutes (every 30 min). The dough will feel tighter and less extensible than pure wheat — do not overwork it. After the folds, leave completely undisturbed. Ready when grown 40–50% with surface bubbles. Rye ferments faster than wheat — do not over-proof.
⏱ 3.5–4.5 hrs
4
Pre-shape — this step is more critical than for wheat
Flour your work surface generously — rye sticks. Scrape the dough out and fold the edges under to form a rough oval. Drag firmly towards you with the bench scraper to tighten the surface. Because rye weakens gluten, you need more tension here than with pure wheat. Cover and rest 30–35 minutes — longer than for wheat, because the rye-weakened gluten needs more time to relax and become extensible for final shaping.
⏱ 30–35 min bench rest
5
Final shape — tight Batard, maximum tension
Flip the dough smooth-side down. Fold top third down, bottom third up. Fold left side to centre, right side over. Roll into a tight oval log — use firm, confident pressure. Drag back with the bench scraper. The surface must be tight. Any slack will result in sideways spread. This dough will not have the silky extensibility of pure wheat — it will feel stiffer and slightly resistant. Work with that resistance rather than against it. Place seam-side UP in a well-floured banneton or bowl.
💡 Flour the banneton more generously than for wheat — rye dough sticks more aggressively. Rice flour mixed with regular flour is excellent for lining if available.
6
Cold proof — longer than wheat
Cover and refrigerate 12–16 hours. The extra cold time is important for Mischbrot — the rye component develops far more of its characteristic flavour during slow cold fermentation. The longer cold proof also firms the loaf more thoroughly, which it needs given the weaker gluten structure.
⏱ 12–16 hrs fridge
7
Bake — slightly lower than wheat, longer time
Preheat stone and bottom tray at 250°C for 45+ minutes. Flip the cold dough onto baking parchment. Score with one long slash, blade at 30–45°. Slide onto stone, pour 250ml boiling water into bottom tray, close oven immediately. After 15 minutes remove the steam tray and reduce to 210°C (slightly lower than wheat — the rye sugars burn faster). Bake a further 28–32 minutes. The crust should be very dark — deep brown with some near-black patches, which is correct and traditional for Mischbrot. Internal temp: 97°C. Cool on a rack for minimum 2 hours before slicing.
⏱ 43–47 min total bake
💡 Same oven-door trick for extra crust: after baking, off oven, door cracked 5cm, leave loaf inside 20 min. The Mischbrot crust becomes genuinely thick — almost like a shell. That's exactly what you want.
